\begin{abstract}
\citet{roelofs2019meta} compared public and private leaderboard scores in 120 Kaggle competitions and found little evidence of substantial adaptive overfitting. We re-test that conclusion on competitions that ended after their sample, with the analysis plan fixed and publicly archived before any new score was read. We first reconstruct the original study from Meta Kaggle. All 120 competitions are recovered and the five accuracy outliers reproduce, but the published test-set sizes do not match the recorded scores for 11 of 32 accuracy competitions; we show how to recover scoring sizes from the scores themselves. We then add a measure, within-team drift, that is unaffected by selection on public score and by fixed differences between the public and private sets. On 487 later competitions, the level of the public--private gap is unchanged for accuracy and AUC competitions. Drift is positive in 68\% of the later competitions and has the same size as in the original sample, about one percent of the distance between a median and a top-decile team. Gap size falls with test-set size, but the part not explained by selection does not. Exploratory analyses show larger gap levels in recent prize competitions, present from teams' first submissions. The 2019 conclusion holds where it can be tested like for like; adaptation to the public leaderboard is real, systematic and small.\footnote{A large language model assistant was used to design and implement the analyses and to draft text, under the authors' direction. The authors reviewed all content and are responsible for it.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A Kaggle competition gives every submission two scores. The public score is computed on one part of the test set and shown at once; the private score is computed on the rest and revealed when the competition ends. The public part is a test set reused thousands of times, the private part is used once, and the difference between them is a natural experiment on adaptive overfitting \citep{dwork2015reusable,blum2015ladder}. \citet{roelofs2019meta} used this design on 120 competitions and concluded that overfitting ``did not pose a significant danger''. Together with test-set replication studies \citep{recht2019imagenet,yadav2019cold}, the result underpins the view that benchmark reuse has been less harmful than theory allows, and it is still cited in that role \citep{sculley2025position}.

The sample behind the result ends in April 2019. Since then the platform has added competitions in which code is re-run on hidden data, a monthly series on synthetic data, and metrics defined per competition. Later descriptive work reports noticeable public--private discrepancies \citep{bonisch2025chronicles,burtch2026monoculture}, though with measures that select each team's best public submission or that are not accompanied by tests. Our searches on 15 and 16 November 2026 found no peer-reviewed re-test of the 2019 result on later competitions; this is a statement about those searches, not proof of absence.

This paper makes four contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\item An independent reconstruction of the 2019 study from the public data, with every discrepancy documented (Section~\ref{sec:recon}).
\item A way to infer test-set sizes from accuracy scores, which extends the original paper's formal test from 19 to 33 of its 34 accuracy competitions and to later competitions without manual collection.
\item Two measures that account for selection: within-team drift, and a top-decile gap adjusted for selection under the original null model (Section~\ref{sec:methods}).
\item A re-test on 487 later competitions with four pre-specified confirmatory tests (Section~\ref{sec:confirm}), followed by secondary and exploratory analyses that are labelled as such.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Adaptive data analysis.} Reusing a holdout set can invalidate its estimates \citep{dwork2015reusable}, and leaderboard mechanisms have been designed to limit this \citep{blum2015ladder}. Theory also predicts when reuse should be benign: similarity between models reduces the effective number of queries \citep{mania2019similarity}, and problems with more classes are harder to overfit by reuse \citep{feldman2019multiclass}.

\paragraph{Empirical studies of reuse.} New test sets for ImageNet and MNIST showed accuracy drops but stable rankings \citep{recht2019imagenet,yadav2019cold}. \citet{roelofs2019meta} is the largest study of reuse within competitions. Its authors listed open questions that motivate our secondary analyses: regression competitions, uncertainty for metrics other than accuracy, and a suggested minimum of 10,000 test examples.

\paragraph{Later analyses of Kaggle.} \citet{bonisch2025chronicles} report normalised public--private discrepancies across competitions, without formal tests. \citet{burtch2026monoculture} reconstructs leaderboards for 158 contests to relate code convergence to the public--private shift of each team's best public submission, and notes that this quantity is biased by selection. Neither re-tests the 2019 analysis.

\section{Reconstructing the original study}
\label{sec:recon}

\paragraph{Sample.} The 120 competition identifiers are listed in the original appendix. All are present in the Meta Kaggle snapshot of 15 November 2026 \citep{metakaggle}. We recover 1,510,406 pre-deadline submissions with both scores, against 1,513,918 published; 46 of the 120 counts match exactly and none differs by more than 2.6\%. The latest deadline in the sample is 4 April 2019, and the next competition that meets the published inclusion rule, with a deadline of 9 April, is absent. We therefore date the original snapshot to those days and start our extension after 4 April 2019.

\paragraph{Inclusion rule.} The published rule is at least 1,000 pre-deadline submissions and one of five metrics. Applied to the current data it yields 28 further competitions inside the original window. Of these, 26 report a public share of 0 or 100 percent, which suggests an unstated requirement that a real split exists.

\paragraph{Headline pattern.} The five largest mean accuracy gaps belong to the five competitions the original paper names as outliers.

\paragraph{Test-set sizes.} Unweighted accuracy times the number of scored examples must be a whole number. For 11 of the 32 accuracy competitions with published sizes, the published sizes fail this check. The clearest case is the Allstate competition. The paper lists 59,657 public and 139,199 private examples, which sum to the 198,856 rows of the test file; the task scores one prediction per customer, and the file contains 55,716 customers. The original text attributes this competition's small $p$-values to its ``198,856 examples''. With customer counts of 16,714 and 39,002, which are the sizes implied by the scores, 1.3\% of submissions have $p \le 0.05$ under the original null model; a normal approximation with the published counts gives 74.6\%. We make no claim about what the original code computed. The other ten cases are four sizes off by one or with public and private exchanged, three with the same total but a different split, two with far fewer scored examples than listed, and one in which every submission has identical public and private scores.

\paragraph{Unspecified choices.} Submission dates have day precision, so the first submission of a team is not always identifiable; the handling of ties at the top-10\% cutoff is not stated; and the tail probability is inclusive in the main text and strict in the appendix. We use submission identifiers to order submissions within a day, keep all ties, and use the inclusive tail.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\paragraph{Cohorts.} $O$ is the original sample. $E$ contains competitions with a deadline after 4 April 2019 and no later than 15 November 2026. $W$ contains earlier regression competitions, which the original study excluded by design. A competition is analysed if it has at least 1,000 pre-deadline submissions, reports a public share between 1 and 99 percent, has at least 1,000 scored submissions from non-benchmark teams, and does not have identical public and private scores for at least 99\% of submissions. This leaves 114 competitions in $O$, 487 in $E$ and 51 in $W$.

\paragraph{Gap.} The gap of a submission is its public score minus its private score, signed so that a positive value favours the public score. The mean gap of a competition is the average over its submissions, as in the original study.

\paragraph{Team spread.} To compare metrics we divide by the team spread: the distance between the median team and the top-decile team, measured on teams' median public scores. A relative gap or drift of 0.01 is one percent of what separates a median team from a top-decile team.

\paragraph{Within-team drift.} For each team with at least four submissions, ordered in time, drift is the mean gap of the later half of its submissions minus the mean gap of the earlier half. The drift of a competition is the average over teams. No submission is chosen by its score, so selection on the public score cannot produce drift, and a constant difference between the public and private sets cancels within each team. A team that tunes to public feedback carries the public noise it has selected into later submissions, which produces positive drift.

\paragraph{Inferred sizes and formal test.} For accuracy competitions we take as public (private) size the smallest integer on which every public (private) score is a whole count. On the original sample this reproduces 21 of 32 published size pairs exactly. With these sizes we compute, for each submission, the exact two-sided inclusive tail probability of its accuracy difference when its correct answers are split at random \citep{roelofs2019meta}.

\paragraph{Selection-adjusted top-decile gap.} The top 10\% of submissions by public score have a positive expected gap even without adaptation. We simulate random splits under the same null model, select the top 10\% on simulated public scores, and subtract the resulting expected gap from the observed one.

\paragraph{Validation.} In a simulation without adaptation, drift averaged $-0.02$ percentage points whether or not the private set was three points harder, while the top-10\% gap was 0.75 and 3.75 points. With adaptation, drift was 1.18 points in both cases. Our exact tail probabilities agree with an independently written implementation on 68,552 submissions to within $10^{-14}$.

\paragraph{Split labels.} From competition descriptions we flagged competitions whose host states that the private set is a later period, a different group or source, or a newly collected dataset: 44 of 516 candidates in $E$. Most descriptions say nothing about the split, so an unflagged competition is not known to have a random split. A re-reading of 50 unflagged competitions with a wider sentence filter changed no label.

\paragraph{Pre-specification.} The measures were developed on $O$, which had been fully examined. The analysis plan, with four confirmatory tests corrected by the method of \citet{holm1979} and six secondary analyses, and the split labels were committed to a public repository and archived by a third party on 16 November 2026, before any score in $E$ or $W$ was read. Until then only counts, metric names, dates and description text of those competitions had been examined. Intervals are 95\% bootstrap intervals over competitions. The competition is the unit of inference throughout.

\section{Secondary results}

These analyses were specified in advance but are not confirmatory.

\paragraph{Regression and classification.} We find no clear difference. Median relative drift is 0.5\% for regression ($E$ and $W$, $n=124$) and 0.7\% for classification ($E$ and $O$, $n=307$); the difference is $-0.2$ points ($-1.0$, $2.1$).

\paragraph{Test-set size.} Among 125 accuracy competitions with inferred sizes, the absolute mean gap falls with total test size (Spearman $-0.61$): its median is 0.83 percentage points below 10,000 examples and 0.16 at or above (Figure~\ref{fig:size}). The selection-adjusted top-decile gap does not vary with size (Spearman 0.12, $p=0.20$). The 10,000-example guideline of the original study is supported as protection against noise. We find no evidence that small test sets add overfitting beyond what selection produces.

\paragraph{Time and platform.} Within $E$ there is no trend in relative drift or relative gap across deadline years. Code-only competitions have larger relative gaps than the rest of $E$ (median 0.30 against 0.007); Section~\ref{sec:explore} examines this.

\paragraph{Formal accuracy test.} The median share of first-per-team submissions with $p \le 0.05$ is 1.7\% in $O$ and 1.8\% in $E$; it exceeds 10\% in 9\% and 11\% of competitions.

\section{Exploratory results}
\label{sec:explore}

The analyses in this section were chosen after the results above were known.

\paragraph{Alternative explanations for drift.} Teams often begin with a trivial submission that has no gap, and gaps might scale with score level. Neither accounts for the result. Drift is positive in 63.3\% of later competitions when only the third and fourth quarters of each team's submissions are compared, in 65.4\% after dropping each team's first two submissions, and in 66.0\% when score level is controlled for within team. Where first submissions have a negative gap, so that inheriting the gap level would produce negative drift, drift is positive in 66.0\%.

\paragraph{The original outliers.} For two of the five outliers the top-10\% gap is what selection alone predicts (excess $-0.23$ and $+0.06$ percentage points). For the other three it is larger ($+6.0$, $+2.8$ and $+1.2$).

\paragraph{Prize competitions.} Among Featured and Research competitions, the median relative gap is 0.17 in $E$ ($n=184$) against 0.01 in $O$ ($n=68$; $p=0.003$), and already 0.08 against 0.01 for teams' first submissions (Figure~\ref{fig:prize}). The share with positive drift is unchanged (72.5\% against 70.6\%). Within $E$, code-only prize competitions do not differ clearly from other prize competitions, so the platform contrast above is mostly one of composition. Only 23 of the 184 use one of the original five metrics, which is why H1 could not detect the pattern. Scores are also more tightly packed in recent prize competitions, which enlarges any gap expressed in team spreads. A gap that is present from the first submission and is not accompanied by more drift points to how public and private sets are built or scored. We have not identified the mechanism.

\section{Limitations}

Drift is evidence of adaptation, not a pure measure of it, and it cannot see adaptation that is complete before a team's first submission, such as starting from a public notebook. The null model treats each submission as independently split, whereas all submissions share one split. Accuracy results rest mostly on in-class competitions in both periods (25 of 33 and 82 of 95), and only two accuracy competitions in $E$ ended after 2022. Split labels record what hosts wrote and were assigned by one reader. Inferred sizes assume unweighted accuracy. The study uses one snapshot of Meta Kaggle. We did not study the number of classes or notebook forking.

\section{Conclusion}

Where the 2019 study can be repeated like for like, its conclusion stands: public and private scores agree as closely after April 2019 as before. Looking within teams shows something the original design could not: in about two thirds of competitions, in both periods, later submissions favour the public leaderboard slightly more than earlier ones. The effect is about one percent of the distance between a median and a top-decile team. Recent prize competitions show larger public-over-private gaps from the first submission onward, which a re-test built on the original metrics cannot explain and which deserves study with competition-level split information.

\subsubsection*{Reproducibility}
Code, the archived analysis plan, the label table and all result tables will be released. All scripts were re-run from the raw files in a clean environment and reproduced every table.
