## Findings, ranked by importance

### 1. Correct the interpretation and domain of Proposition 1

**Type: mathematical interpretation error; the displayed identity and proof themselves are sound. Fix before submission.**

Evidence: `paper/main.tex:78`, following Proposition 1, says drift can indicate adaptation only where concentration of improvement on public examples is excluded, “for instance by a verified random split”. A verified random split does not exclude realized concentration. It supplies a randomization distribution under an appropriate fixed-submission null. The proposition proves zero expectation, not that realized drift is zero, nor that every positive realization is adaptive.

The paper's own five-example construction is enough: take per-example improvements `(1,1,1,1,0)`, with one uniformly selected public example. Exact enumeration gives drift `(0.25,0.25,0.25,0.25,-1)`, mean zero and positive sign in 80% of splits, with no feedback. I independently enumerated it this round. The text's final qualification about the sign helps, but does not repair the preceding assertion that randomization excludes concentration.

**Suggested replacement:** “For fixed submissions, drift records a difference in mean improvement between the two sets. A verified random split provides a zero-expectation reference and, with suitable information, a randomization distribution; positive realized drift alone still does not establish adaptation.” Retain the distinction between an expectation and a fair-sign reference. Avoid presenting random splitting as a sufficient causal identification condition by itself.

Two related scope corrections:

- `paper/main.tex:228` says “Neither holds” for non-example-averaged metrics such as AUC or clipped scores. This is stronger than the proof establishes. Say **“This proof does not establish these claims for…”** instead. Equal-size split exchangeability can give zero expected drift for AUC too, when scores are well-defined. An exact check with six labelled examples and 18 valid equal-size splits gave nonzero drift values in symmetric pairs and mean zero. That is a counterexample to a categorical denial, not a proposed extension of the theorem.
- The conclusion at `paper/main.tex:207` invokes the per-example-improvement identity for the all-metric divergence without repeating the additive/unclipped restriction. Add “for example-averaged, unclipped metrics” at that invocation. AUC, RMSE and the clipped measures should not silently inherit the proposition.

For the competition-level extension, mean-zero carries over by linearity with fixed participating teams and weights. The realized identity is for the **team-averaged** per-example improvement; it does not imply that every individual team has positive drift. That qualification can be made in a short phrase.

### 2. The strengthened abstract loses an essential qualification of the Allstate calculation

**Type: consequential reporting ambiguity, not an error in the saved numbers. Fix before submission.**

Evidence: `paper/main.tex:23` compares 1.3% and 74.6% as rejection rates of the original null model, and says whether the competition shows irregularity depends on the scoring unit. The reconstruction section (`:59`) is more accurate: 74.6% is a **normal-approximation sensitivity diagnostic**, and the original code was unavailable. The abstract can be read as a recovered original result or the same exact test computed twice.

The existing verification note already offers a clean like-for-like presentation:

| Scoring counts and calculation | Fraction at p ≤ 0.05 |
|---|---:|
| Customer counts, exact inclusive tail | 1.285% |
| Customer counts, normal approximation | 1.313% |
| Published row counts, normal approximation | 74.620% |

Source: `outputs/allstate_verification_note.md` and `outputs/stage1/allstate_file_verification.json`. I freshly recounted the downloaded test file: **198,856 rows, 55,716 customers, zero missing customer IDs**. The scoring-unit discrepancy remains well supported.

**Fix:** identify the comparison as a sensitivity calculation using the same normal approximation (1.3% versus 74.6% after rounding), or retain the exact-versus-normal comparison with both methods named. For example: “A normal-approximation sensitivity calculation changes the share of submissions below p=0.05 from 74.6% with the published row counts to 1.3% with customer counts.” Keep the exact calculation in the reconstruction section and retain the disclaimer about unavailable original code.

Prefer “changes the submission-level rejection fraction” to “decides the outcome of the formal test for the competition.” No calibrated single competition-level decision rule for that rejection fraction has been established. This correction preserves the force of the finding without claiming an original implementation error or a new competition-level hypothesis test.

### 3. Narrow two new identification statements

**Type: overclaim; fix before submission with wording, not new experiments.**

**Simulation:** `paper/main.tex:158` says within-team drift “does not tell the two apart”. The illustration establishes that **positive drift of similar magnitude can arise through either mechanism**. The two simulated outcomes are not identical: mean drift is 0.558455 versus 0.658672 percentage points, and positive-team shares are 92.67% versus 83%. No equality of their distributions or inability of every drift-based procedure to distinguish these specified generators was shown. The contribution bullet already uses the better formulation, “positive drift is not specific to adaptation”. Use that formulation consistently. “The statistic our data allow” is also unnecessarily exclusive: the project computes several statistics.

The simulation is a useful explanatory illustration. I reran it and reproduced every numerical summary exactly. It does not need parameter tuning to force identical outputs or a larger simulation campaign. Its “no real improvement” description is a stipulated toy mechanism, not a measured generalization result: the code generates correctness vectors rather than fitted models and fresh-test performance. State that plainly if discussing population performance.

**Required data:** `paper/main.tex:207` says settling these questions needs, “at a minimum”, per-example predictions and split assignments. Those are useful for the proposed resplitting/composition analyses, but are not universally necessary for causal identification. For example, randomized access to feedback can identify an average effect on a specified aggregate outcome under a suitable design, even if the investigator receives only aggregate outcomes. The next sentence already recognizes randomized feedback access.

**Fix:** “Per-example predictions and documented split assignments would enable stronger checks of sampling, composition and dependence; causal attribution additionally requires a justified design or explicit identifying assumptions.” Avoid universal necessary-data claims. The practical recommendation remains worthwhile.

### 4. Update the reproducibility scope for the newly added illustration

The new `scripts/illustrate_identification.py` was added after those runs, is not in `run_all.sh`, and does not read raw data. The revised appendix generator also postdates the recorded production run. The 2,443-cell comparison describes the earlier pipeline, not the new illustration.

**Fix:** change this to “The empirical analysis pipeline was rerun…” and describe the fixed-seed illustration and appendix generation separately. Do not edit historical manifests to pretend they cover later scripts. The old pipeline evidence is valid; expanding its stated coverage is not.

The anonymous README should include the separate illustration/appendix commands and identify what the pipeline comparison covers. No full raw-data rebuild is warranted for these reporting changes. If the final manuscript claims two-environment verification of the new illustration, actually perform and record that check first; otherwise keep the narrower truthful claim.
